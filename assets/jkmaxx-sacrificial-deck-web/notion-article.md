If your review room stays polite, you are not learning anything. People say “interesting” when they mean “risky.” They say “later” when they mean “never.” The real constraints stay unspoken until something concrete is on the table.

That is the job of a **sacrificial concept**: a direction you are willing to lose, because it forces the org to say what it will not put in a slide header.

It is not sloppy work dressed up as boldness. It is a high-quality hypothesis whose purpose is to **surface trade-offs fast** so the decision you ship is aligned with reality, not hope.

This deck is a **sacrificial concept exercise** in the consulting sense: a set of deliberately wide provocations used to stretch the brief before you converge. That is why the ideas below look “too much” on purpose.

## Publishing design insights from consulting projects

As a senior designer in consulting, you work across industries, but public writing rewards **one sharp thesis** readers can reuse. The job is not to recap the deck. It is to extract a **portable method** someone can take into their next review.

**Pressure-test before you post (a simple research pass)**

1. **Saturation** — Has this exact angle already been said to death on LinkedIn and in design pubs? If yes, you need a sharper **differentiation** hook, not louder adjectives.
2. **Proof from other industries** — Where else has the same move shown up? Borrow examples to show the pattern is real, not a one-off slide.
3. **Language** — What is the cleanest existing framing? Build on it or deliberately extend it. Do not invent jargon for its own sake.

**What a sweep of this deck suggested**

- The “white space” is often at the **intersection** of methods (for example culture + invisible problems, or reframe + data + service), not in naming a single technique everyone already quotes.
- Some angles are **crowded** when stated generically (for example “GenAI in design” without a crisp decision model). If you publish there, lead with **what you refuse to automate** and **what you multiply**, not a trend headline.
- **Evergreen craft pieces** still land when they teach a named practice readers can use Monday morning. That is the lane for **sacrificial concepts**.

**What readers reward (same bar as a senior case study)**

1. **Why the problem mattered** in business and user terms.
2. **What you owned** versus what the team owned.
3. **Constraints** that shaped the work: regulation, tech debt, channel reality, politics, time.
4. **Key decisions**, not a screen tour: options, why alternatives lost, evidence.
5. **Impact**, even when numbers are confidential — say so, then use directional outcomes or qualitative shifts.

**The drafting mistake to avoid**

Feeding raw slides to a model and asking for “a LinkedIn post.” You get generic polish. The fix: **you** supply decisions, stakeholder dynamics, and constraints; the draft is only compression.

**What needs extra care**

Identifying details, roadmaps, unreleased concepts, and metrics. When in doubt, ask. Teach the **method** first; treat visuals as **illustrative**, not as a data room.

## The pattern I use in hard reviews

1. **Anchor** with something credible today (cost, ops, distribution).
2. **Stretch** with a premium story that still feels commercially legible.
3. **Probe** with a frontier idea that makes objections explicit.

The “probe” is often the one that dies in the meeting. If it did its job, it still wins.

## Senior designer takeaway

**Sacrifice is not about losing. It is about owning the decision quality of the system.**

**Publishing** is the same muscle: own the narrative, show how you think under constraints, skip the theater.

If you want a LinkedIn-native version, cut the long essay to ~800 characters, keep **three** slides that represent anchor / stretch / probe, and lead with **one** decision you are willing to defend in the comments.
